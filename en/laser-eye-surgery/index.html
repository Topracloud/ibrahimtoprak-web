Laser Eye Surgery: Methods and Candidacy Assessment

Laser vision correction is not suitable for everyone. This guide explains the differences between PRK, No-Touch, LASIK and SMILE, who should not have laser, the Pentacam-based preoperative evaluation, ectasia risk, and the phakic intraocular lens option for those who are not laser candidates.

What is laser eye surgery?

Laser eye surgery (laser vision correction) uses an excimer or femtosecond laser to reshape the cornea at the micron level, correcting myopia, hyperopia and astigmatism and reducing dependence on glasses and contact lenses. It takes about 10–15 minutes for both eyes, is performed with anesthetic drops and requires no hospital stay.

The laser removes a calculated amount of corneal tissue so that light focuses correctly on the retina. In myopia the central cornea is flattened; in hyperopia tissue is removed peripherally to steepen the center; in astigmatism the difference in curvature is balanced. Removed tissue does not grow back, so it is essential to confirm beforehand that the cornea is thick and strong enough to give up that tissue safely. The factor that most determines the long-term outcome of laser eye surgery is not the device but correct patient selection.

Differences between PRK, No-Touch, LASIK and SMILE

All four methods correct the prescription by reshaping the cornea; they differ in which corneal layer the laser reaches and how. PRK and No-Touch (TransPRK) work on the surface, LASIK lifts a thin flap, and SMILE removes a lens-shaped piece of tissue through a small incision. The choice depends on corneal thickness, prescription, dry eye, occupation and lifestyle.

On modern platforms all methods can be customized: wavefront-guided treatment accounts for the eye's optical aberrations, while topography-guided treatment accounts for irregularities of the corneal surface. Topography-guided treatment is preferred in irregular corneas and in people who have had previous laser surgery.

Who is a candidate for laser eye surgery?

  • Age 18 or over (preferably 21+) with a stable prescription for at least one year
  • Myopia roughly −1 to −8/−10 D, hyperopia +1 to +4 D, astigmatism up to 5–6 D (depending on the platform and cornea)
  • Normal corneal thickness and topography, leaving enough safe tissue after treatment
  • No active eye disease (keratoconus, advanced dry eye, uncontrolled glaucoma, active infection)
  • Not pregnant or breastfeeding
  • Realistic expectations: laser reduces dependence on glasses; it does not prevent reading glasses after 40 (presbyopia)

Who is not a candidate for laser eye surgery?

Laser is not performed in people with thin or irregular corneas, keratoconus or suspected keratoconus, a prescription that is still changing, age under 18, pregnancy, uncontrolled dry eye or allergy, certain rheumatic or autoimmune diseases, or a prescription beyond what the corneal thickness allows. For these patients alternatives such as phakic intraocular lenses are considered.

  • Corneal: thin cornea, irregular or asymmetric topography, keratoconus or forme fruste keratoconus, previous corneal disease or scarring, some corneal dystrophies
  • Prescription: changes within the past year; prescriptions the corneal thickness cannot accommodate
  • Ocular surface: dry eye not controlled with treatment, severe allergic conjunctivitis, eyelid inflammation
  • Systemic: uncontrolled diabetes, active autoimmune or rheumatic disease, medications that impair wound healing, pregnancy and breastfeeding
  • Other eye disease: advanced glaucoma, cataract (cataract surgery with a lens implant is then considered), active retinal disease
  • Expectations: anyone expecting a guaranteed "zero" prescription; a fine-tuning procedure may be needed in some patients

Not being a candidate is often not permanent; re-evaluation is possible once dry eye is treated, the prescription stabilizes or allergy is controlled. If the corneal structure itself is unsuitable, the right answer is not laser but a phakic intraocular lens.

Pre-laser Pentacam evaluation and preoperative assessment

Corneal tomography (Pentacam) is at the center of the pre-laser evaluation: front and back surface curvature, elevation maps, thickness distribution and combined risk indices (such as BAD-D) are measured. Epithelial mapping, dry eye testing, pupil size, dilated fundus examination and confirmation of the prescription with cycloplegic (dilating) drops are added. The aim is to select corneas on which laser can be applied safely and to predict the risk of ectasia in advance.

What the assessment includes

  1. Stopping contact lenses: soft lenses at least 1 week and rigid lenses 3–4 weeks beforehand; otherwise the corneal shape is measured incorrectly.
  2. Refraction and cycloplegic examination: the true prescription is confirmed with drops that relax the eye's focusing.
  3. Corneal tomography: front/back surfaces, thickness map, thinnest point, back surface elevation, asymmetry indices.
  4. Epithelial map: the epithelium can hide irregularity beneath it; its thickness distribution can reveal early keratoconus.
  5. Ocular surface and dry eye: tear break-up time, Schirmer test, meibomian gland assessment. Dry eye is the most common complaint after laser and should be treated beforehand.
  6. Pupil size and wavefront (aberrometry): to predict night halos and glare.
  7. Fundus, eye pressure and slit-lamp examination: to exclude retinal and glaucoma problems.

All of these data are interpreted together to decide which method (PRK/No-Touch, LASIK, SMILE) or non-laser option is appropriate. If a finding is borderline, repeating the measurements after a few months is usually wiser than proceeding.

Corneal thickness and ectasia risk

Ectasia is weakening and keratoconus-like bulging of the cornea months to years after laser; it is the most serious long-term complication of laser surgery and is rare. The main risk factors are unrecognized early keratoconus before surgery, a thin cornea, a large amount of tissue removal for a high prescription, an inadequate residual stromal bed and young age. Modern assessment combines these factors into an individual risk score; stating that "the cornea is over 500 microns" is not sufficient on its own. Our 2015 study proposing a diagnostic criterion that evaluates the cornea's steepest and thinnest points together, usable in pre-laser screening, is an example of this approach (see research). When the risk is high, a phakic IOL is recommended instead of laser; when it is borderline, a surface laser without a flap (PRK/No-Touch) may be preferred over LASIK.

People with suspected keratoconus

Standard laser eye surgery is not performed on an eye with diagnosed or suspected keratoconus. Because laser removes corneal tissue, it further thins a weak cornea and can trigger the disease. The most important purpose of the pre-laser evaluation is to identify people with subclinical keratoconus, the stage without symptoms.

Subclinical keratoconus is a cornea that appears normal on a glasses examination but shows early changes on tomography in back surface elevation, thickness distribution or the epithelial map. If these patients undergo surgery, the risk of ectasia is markedly higher. Our recommendation for anyone with suspicious findings: either forgo laser, or repeat measurements after 6–12 months to check for progression; if independence from glasses is desired, discuss a phakic IOL, which leaves the corneal tissue untouched. For detailed information see the keratoconus guide.

Hyperopia and refractive surgery

In hyperopia (farsightedness) the laser removes tissue from the corneal periphery to steepen the center. There are differences from myopia: the treatable range is narrower (generally up to +4 D), regression (partial return of the prescription) is more common, and because the treatment zone must be wide, corneal diameter and pupil size matter more. In young hyperopes, latent hyperopia must be uncovered with a cycloplegic examination to avoid undercorrection. In hyperopes over 40–45, refractive lens exchange may be a more lasting solution than laser; in high hyperopia, hyperopic phakic lenses are considered.

Phakic intraocular lens (ICL): the alternative to laser

A phakic intraocular lens (ICL and similar) is placed between the iris and the natural lens without removing the natural lens, working like a permanent contact lens inside the eye. Because no corneal tissue is removed, it is the main alternative for independence from glasses in thin or irregular corneas, high myopia (above −8/−10 D), significant dry eye and other situations where laser is unsuitable. It can be removed if necessary.

Candidacy requires measuring the anterior chamber depth and the distance between the iris and the lens; if this space is insufficient, the lens cannot be implanted. Surgery takes 10–15 minutes under topical or local anesthesia; vision usually recovers substantially by the next day. Toric models also correct astigmatism. Possible risks include a temporary rise in eye pressure, cataract development in the long term and the need to reposition the lens; regular follow-up is required. Toric phakic lenses can also be considered in selected patients with stable keratoconus who want independence from glasses.

Surgery day and recovery

On the morning of surgery, no makeup or perfume; a companion is needed to drive. Anesthetic drops are applied; you are asked to look at a light, and the device tracks eye movements. The laser itself takes only seconds per eye. With LASIK, vision is blurry the same day and largely clear the next; with PRK and No-Touch there is stinging, tearing and light sensitivity for the first 3–4 days, the bandage lens is removed on day 4–5 and vision settles over 1–4 weeks. In the first week eye rubbing, swimming and dust are to be avoided, and drops are used as prescribed. Follow-up visits are typically at day 1, week 1, month 1 and months 3–6.

Risks and side effects

Laser eye surgery is widely performed with a well-characterized safety profile, but like any surgery it has risks: temporary or persistent dry eye, halos and glare around lights at night, under- or overcorrection requiring an enhancement, partial regression of the prescription over time, flap-related problems specific to LASIK, temporary surface haze specific to PRK, infection and, very rarely, ectasia. Most of these risks can be reduced by correct patient selection and by treating dry eye and allergy before surgery. Results vary from person to person; no method can guarantee a "zero" prescription.

Frequently asked questions

Is laser eye surgery safe?

Laser refractive surgery is one of the most frequently performed eye operations worldwide and its safety profile in properly selected patients is well established. Most of the risk is identified at the preoperative evaluation; not operating on thin or irregular corneas is the basis of long-term safety.

Which is better, No-Touch laser or LASIK?

Neither is absolutely superior; visual results are comparable. No-Touch/PRK creates no flap, preserves corneal integrity and is preferred for thin corneas and contact sports, but recovery is slower and the first days are more uncomfortable. LASIK gives clear vision the next day but carries flap-related and early dry-eye risks. The choice follows corneal measurements and lifestyle.

At what age can I have laser eye surgery?

The lower limit is 18; many surgeons prefer 21 so the prescription has stabilized. There is no upper limit, but after 40–45 reading glasses (presbyopia) will be needed and cataract approaches, so intraocular lens options are considered alongside laser in this age group.

Can people with astigmatism have laser?

Yes. Regular astigmatism can be corrected with all laser methods. Irregular astigmatism (asymmetry on topography), however, may indicate keratoconus, in which case laser is not performed. Corneal tomography is essential to distinguish the two.

What prescription is too high for laser?

There is no fixed number; corneal thickness sets the limit. In general, myopia above −8/−10 D, hyperopia above +4 D and astigmatism above 5–6 D may be better served by a phakic IOL. Even a low prescription is not treated with laser if the cornea is thin.

Will my prescription come back after laser?

Stability is high for low-to-moderate prescriptions. Partial regression can occur in high myopia and hyperopia, in young patients and in those operated on before the prescription stabilized, which is why at least a year of stability is required. If regression occurs and enough cornea remains, an enhancement can be performed.

Will I need reading glasses after laser when I turn 40?

Yes. Laser corrects the cornea; the difficulty with near vision that begins around 40–45 (presbyopia) is due to the natural lens losing flexibility and develops independently of laser. You will be glasses-free for distance but may need reading glasses. A monovision approach can be discussed for some patients.

How is keratoconus screening done before laser eye surgery?

Corneal tomography (Pentacam) evaluates the front and back surfaces, the thickness map and elevation data; epithelial mapping is added. Even if a single measurement looks normal, these data are read together. If a finding is suspicious, laser is postponed and the measurements are repeated after a few months.
