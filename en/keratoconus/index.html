Keratoconus: Symptoms, Early Diagnosis and Current Treatments

Keratoconus is a progressive disease in which the cornea thins and bulges, distorting vision. This guide explains who gets it, its early symptoms, how it is diagnosed with Pentacam and epithelial mapping, and current treatments led by corneal cross-linking, in plain language.

What is keratoconus?

Keratoconus is a progressive corneal disease in which the cornea, the clear front window of the eye, gradually thins and bulges forward into a cone shape, distorting vision. It usually begins in the teenage years, can progress into the 30s and 40s, and if not recognized in time may lead to vision loss that glasses cannot correct and, in advanced cases, to corneal transplantation.

A healthy cornea is a smooth dome that focuses light sharply onto the retina. In keratoconus the corneal tissue weakens; under the eye's internal pressure the weakest point bulges forward. This irregular surface creates an astigmatism that glasses cannot fully correct (irregular astigmatism). The disease usually affects both eyes, often to very different degrees.

Who gets keratoconus? Risk factors

Keratoconus is more common than generally assumed, affecting roughly 1 in 500 to 1 in 2,000 people in screening studies, with higher rates reported in Mediterranean and Middle Eastern populations, including Turkey. There is no single cause; genetic predisposition and environmental factors act together.

Eye rubbing

Eye rubbing is the best-documented modifiable risk factor. Forceful, repeated rubbing puts mechanical stress on the cornea and can accelerate bulging in a predisposed eye. People with allergic conjunctivitis, vernal keratoconjunctivitis, eczema or dry eye who rub constantly are at particular risk. The first recommendation for anyone diagnosed with keratoconus is to stop rubbing entirely and to treat the cause of the itching.

Family history

Having a first-degree relative with keratoconus raises the risk above that of the general population. Siblings and children of patients should be screened with corneal tomography even if they have no complaints.

Sleeping face-down and mechanical pressure

Sleeping on the stomach or on one side with the eye pressed against the pillow or arm applies pressure to the cornea all night. In markedly asymmetric or one-sided keratoconus, sleeping position should be asked about. Improperly fitted rigid contact lenses can also load the cornea mechanically.

Associated conditions

  • Allergic disease: asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis
  • Down syndrome and some connective tissue disorders (Ehlers-Danlos, Marfan)
  • Conditions that lead to eye rubbing, such as retinitis pigmentosa or Leber congenital amaurosis
  • Sleep apnea and other situations involving frequent eye rubbing

Early symptoms of keratoconus

The most common early sign is a glasses prescription that keeps changing and getting stronger, especially increasing astigmatism. Blurred vision despite new glasses, halos and streaks around lights, difficulty seeing at night, ghost or double images with one eye, and a history of frequent eye rubbing should raise suspicion of early keratoconus.

  • Several prescription changes within 1–2 years
  • New glasses that never seem "right"
  • Starbursts or comet-like streaks around lights at night
  • A large difference between the two eyes
  • Rapidly increasing astigmatism during adolescence
  • Family history of keratoconus or habitual eye rubbing

In advanced disease the cornea bulges visibly, fine lines and scarring can develop, and rarely a tear in the inner corneal layer causes sudden clouding and pain (acute hydrops).

Diagnosis: Pentacam, corneal tomography and epithelial mapping

Keratoconus is diagnosed with corneal tomography. Scheimpflug-based devices such as the Pentacam measure the curvature of the front and back corneal surfaces, the thickness map and elevation data; epithelial mapping shows the pattern of thinning and thickening in the cornea's outermost layer. These measurements can reveal the disease years before a routine glasses examination shows anything abnormal.

What is examined

  • Front and back surface curvature (K values, Kmax): the steepest point and its location
  • Thickness map: the thinnest point and its distance from the center; how quickly thinning spreads
  • Back surface elevation: often the first place the disease shows, before the front surface changes
  • Epithelial map: thinning over the cone apex with surrounding thickening (a "doughnut" pattern). The epithelium smooths over the irregularity beneath it and can mask the front surface; the epithelial map removes that mask.
  • Combined indices (BAD-D, ABCD staging): systems that combine several measurements into a single risk score
  • Corneal biomechanics and densitometry: additional data on tissue stiffness and clarity in selected cases

No single measurement makes the diagnosis; in early disease each value may look borderline and becomes meaningful only when read together. Diagnosing subclinical keratoconus, the stage without symptoms, therefore requires experience and an integrated reading of the data. Our research examines how new markers such as the cornea's three-dimensional geometry and light scattering in the epithelium can contribute to earlier detection (see research).

Does keratoconus progress? Follow-up and progression criteria

Keratoconus does not progress at the same rate in everyone. The tendency to progress is highest in adolescence and young adulthood; after age 35–40 most patients slow down or stabilize. Age alone is no guarantee, however; if triggers such as eye rubbing continue, progression can occur later in life.

Progression is determined by comparing serial tomography: an increase in Kmax, a decrease in the thinnest corneal thickness, an increase in back surface elevation and a meaningful change in refraction are the main criteria. Follow-up intervals depend on age and disease speed; every 3–6 months in teenagers and every 6–12 months in stable adults is a common approach. The decision to treat, particularly with cross-linking, is based on documented progression or, in young patients at high risk, as a preventive measure.

Keratoconus treatment: current options

Keratoconus treatment has two goals: stopping progression (cross-linking) and improving vision (glasses, specialty contact lenses, intracorneal ring segments, topography-guided laser and, in advanced disease, corneal transplantation). Which options are combined depends on the stage, corneal thickness, age and visual needs, and is planned individually.

Glasses and contact lenses

Glasses may suffice early on. As irregular astigmatism increases, rigid gas-permeable (RGP) lenses, hybrid lenses or scleral lenses that rest on the white of the eye without touching the cornea can significantly improve vision. Contact lenses do not stop the disease; they only correct vision.

Intracorneal ring segments (ICRS)

Semicircular implants placed in a femtosecond-laser-created channel flatten the cornea, reduce irregular astigmatism and may improve contact lens tolerance. They are usually considered together with or after cross-linking in moderate-stage patients with a clear central cornea. Results vary with the patient and stage.

Corneal transplantation (keratoplasty)

Transplantation is considered when central scarring develops or when contact lenses no longer provide adequate vision. With early diagnosis and cross-linking, the need for transplantation has decreased markedly.

Corneal cross-linking (CXL)

Corneal cross-linking strengthens the cornea by applying riboflavin (vitamin B2) drops followed by controlled UV-A light, creating new bonds between collagen fibers. Its purpose is to stop keratoconus from progressing; it is not designed to eliminate the glasses prescription. It is performed in patients with documented progression or a high risk of progression.

How it is done

The procedure is done under anesthetic drops in an operating-room setting and takes about 30–60 minutes. In the standard (epithelium-off) protocol the outer epithelial layer is removed, riboflavin is absorbed and UV-A is applied. Accelerated protocols shorten the illumination time. A bandage contact lens is worn for a few days; stinging, tearing and light sensitivity are normal for the first 3–5 days, and vision recovers over a few weeks.

Cross-linking in thin corneas

The standard protocol requires a minimum corneal thickness to protect the inner corneal layer from UV. Patients below that threshold are often told that cross-linking "cannot be done." In fact, protocols that adjust riboflavin application, light dose and duration to the individual cornea (thin-cornea and individualized approaches) allow safe treatment in a substantial proportion of these patients. The decision rests on a detailed assessment of the thickness map.

Athens and Cretan protocols: cross-linking that also improves vision

The Athens protocol combines cross-linking with a limited, topography-guided surface laser (PRK) in the same session. The aim is to strengthen the cornea while partially regularizing the irregular surface to improve visual quality, not to eliminate glasses. The Cretan protocol removes the epithelium with laser (PTK) before cross-linking, using the epithelium's own irregularity to smooth the surface. These protocols are considered in selected patients with adequate corneal thickness, documented progression and inadequate vision with glasses. Published studies report that these combined approaches have greater potential for visual improvement than standard cross-linking alone; results vary from patient to patient.

Which protocol for whom?

Corneal thickness, cone location, stage, age and current vision are evaluated together. Two patients with the same diagnosis may receive different protocols; tomography data decides the difference.

Can keratoconus patients have laser eye surgery?

Standard laser vision correction (LASIK, standard PRK, SMILE) is not performed on an eye with keratoconus; these procedures remove tissue and would further thin an already weak cornea and accelerate the disease. However, in selected stable or cross-linking-stabilized eyes, a limited topography-guided surface laser (Athens protocol) may be planned to improve visual quality. This is a vision-quality procedure, not a "get rid of glasses" operation.

The same caution applies to people with suspected but unconfirmed (subclinical) keratoconus. Identifying them during pre-laser screening is the single most important step in preventing post-laser ectasia (corneal weakening). See the laser eye surgery page. For keratoconus patients who want independence from glasses and are not laser candidates, phakic intraocular lenses (toric ICL) may be an option once the disease is stable.

Keratoconus in children and teenagers

Keratoconus progresses faster in childhood and tends to be more advanced at diagnosis than in adults. Families whose child has been diagnosed should know three things:

  1. Time matters. In teenagers progression can occur within months; follow-up is more frequent and cross-linking is not delayed once progression is seen. Many centers discuss treatment at diagnosis without waiting for progression.
  2. Eye rubbing must stop. Allergy is treated; protective eye shields at night may be needed.
  3. Siblings should be screened. Family history is one of the strongest risk factors.

Frequently asked questions

Can keratoconus be cured?

Keratoconus cannot currently be reversed; the cornea does not return to its original shape. Progression can, however, be largely stopped with cross-linking, and vision can be improved with glasses, specialty lenses, intracorneal rings or topography-guided laser. The great majority of patients diagnosed early never need a corneal transplant.

Is keratoconus caused by eye rubbing?

Rubbing is not the sole cause, but it is the best-documented trigger. In a genetically predisposed cornea, forceful and repeated rubbing can bring the disease forward and accelerate it. Treating the allergy that causes itching and stopping rubbing completely are essential.

Is cross-linking painful? How long does it take?

The procedure is performed with anesthetic drops and is not painful during treatment; it takes 30–60 minutes. Because the epithelium is removed, there is stinging, tearing and light sensitivity for the first 2–4 days, managed with a bandage lens and drops. Vision recovers over a few weeks.

Will my prescription go away after cross-linking?

No. The goal of cross-linking is to stop the disease, not to remove the prescription. Some flattening and a small improvement may occur over time, but this is a bonus rather than an expectation. Combined approaches such as the Athens protocol can be planned separately to improve vision.

I was told my cornea is too thin for cross-linking. Are there options?

The standard protocol requires a minimum thickness, but thin-cornea and individualized protocols allow safe treatment in a substantial proportion of these patients. A detailed assessment of the thickness map determines whether an approach suitable for your cornea exists.

Could a keratoconus diagnosis be wrong? Should I get a second opinion?

Early keratoconus can be confused with high astigmatism, contact-lens-induced corneal warpage or surface irregularity from dry eye. Diagnosis should rest on reading the front and back surfaces, thickness and epithelial maps together, and if necessary on repeated measurements a few months apart. A second opinion before any surgical decision is reasonable.

Can keratoconus patients wear contact lenses?

Yes. Soft toric lenses early on and rigid gas-permeable, hybrid or scleral lenses in later stages can markedly improve vision. Lenses do not worsen the disease, but poorly fitted rigid lenses can load the cornea mechanically, so fitting should be done by a specialist.

Is keratoconus hereditary?

Keratoconus has a genetic component; the risk is higher in first-degree relatives, but it does not pass to every child. We recommend screening the children of patients with corneal tomography during adolescence even without symptoms.
