Keratoconus in Children

My Child Was Diagnosed With Keratoconus. What Now?

Keratoconus progresses faster in children and teenagers than in adults, but when caught early it can largely be stopped. This guide is written for parents who have just received the diagnosis: first steps, confirming the diagnosis, treatment timing, and what to do at home and at school.

The first 24 hours: no panic, but a plan

If your child has just been diagnosed with keratoconus, the first thing to know is this: when caught early, keratoconus is a disease whose progression can largely be stopped. It does not cause blindness; left untreated it can seriously impair vision, but treated, most children go on to live normal lives. There are three things to do: stop eye rubbing starting today, confirm the diagnosis and stage with corneal tomography, and, if the disease is progressing, not delay treatment.

What parents feel most after that first visit is uncertainty: "How bad is it? Will there be surgery? Are glasses enough?" This page answers each of those questions in plain language. If you want to start with what keratoconus is, read the keratoconus guide first; here we focus only on what is specific to children and teenagers.

Why is keratoconus different in children?

In children and teenagers keratoconus progresses faster than in adults, tends to be at a more advanced stage when diagnosed, and can worsen noticeably within a single year. The "wait and see" window is therefore short: follow-up is more frequent and the treatment decision is made earlier.

There are several reasons. The young cornea is more elastic and deforms more easily under intraocular pressure. Hormonal changes of puberty affect corneal structure. Allergic disease, and with it eye rubbing, is more common at this age. And children notice visual blur late or do not report it; a child who sees well with one eye may not notice deterioration in the other for years. Keratoconus in children therefore often surfaces as glasses that "never seem right" or an asymmetry picked up at a school screening.

Is the diagnosis right? Do we need a second opinion?

In children, keratoconus is not diagnosed from a glasses exam alone; it requires corneal tomography (Pentacam or similar) assessing the front and back corneal surfaces, the thickness map and, where possible, the epithelial map. If your child could not cooperate well, the scan quality was low, or the diagnosis rests on a single image, repeating the measurements within a few weeks, and a second opinion if needed, is reasonable.

Two situations make diagnosis harder in children. First, a healthy cornea with high astigmatism can mimic early keratoconus. Second, when a child cannot fixate on the device, blinks, or arrives having rubbed the eyes, the maps can be misleading. So before the visit, make sure your child does not rub the eyes that morning, stops contact lenses at least a week earlier if they wear them, and ask that the scans be taken calmly and repeated if necessary.

Bring with you

Any tomography or topography printouts from another center. In keratoconus the most valuable information is not a single scan but change over time; an older map can tell us whether the disease is progressing without waiting months.

What the examination looks at

  • Best vision with glasses: whether a child who does not reach 20/20 with glasses has irregular astigmatism
  • Corneal tomography: front/back surface steepness (Kmax), the thinnest point and its location, back surface elevation
  • Epithelial map: thinning over the cone apex with surrounding thickening; unmasking the front surface in early disease
  • Difference between the two eyes: keratoconus is usually asymmetric; the "good" eye is examined just as carefully
  • Allergy and itching history: hay fever, eczema, asthma; how often and how the eyes are rubbed
  • Sleeping position: face-down or with the eye pressed into the pillow
  • Family history: keratoconus in parents or siblings

With these data the disease is staged and the key question is answered: Is this cornea progressing right now, and how high is the risk? The treatment decision follows from that answer.

Treatment: when, and which treatment?

Treatment in children has two parts. First, stopping the disease: corneal cross-linking in children with documented progression or a high risk of it. Second, correcting vision: glasses, specialty contact lenses when needed, and intracorneal ring segments in advanced cases. In children the cross-linking decision is made earlier than in adults; many centers discuss treatment in a teenager at diagnosis, without waiting for progression to be documented.

Are glasses enough?

Early on, usually yes; glasses correct vision but do not stop the disease. When glasses no longer give adequate vision, rigid gas-permeable, hybrid or scleral lenses are considered. Children can wear these lenses, but hygiene and fitting follow-up are essential.

When is cross-linking done?

An increase in Kmax on serial scans, corneal thinning, rising back surface elevation or a meaningful change in refraction are signs of progression. Because progression can be fast in children, one of these signs is usually enough to treat. In some situations, for example when the other eye has progressed rapidly, preventive treatment may be recommended before progression is documented.

Intracorneal rings and transplantation

Intracorneal ring segments (ICRS) are considered in selected cases to improve visual quality after cross-linking. Corneal transplantation is rarely needed in children and is almost entirely preventable with early diagnosis.

Cross-linking in children: what parents ask

Cross-linking in children follows the same principle as in adults: riboflavin drops are applied to the cornea followed by controlled UV-A light. The differences are that in young children the procedure may be done under general anesthesia, and that eye rubbing must be prevented more strictly during recovery. The procedure takes 30–60 minutes, the child goes home the same day, and there is stinging and light sensitivity for the first 3–5 days.

  • Anesthesia: anesthetic drops are sufficient in cooperative teenagers; general anesthesia may be preferred for young children and anxious patients. The decision is individual.
  • Both eyes: if both eyes are progressing, they can be treated in the same session or a short interval apart.
  • School: one week off is usually enough; screens and reading are uncomfortable in the first days.
  • Thin cornea: for children below the thickness limit of the standard protocol, thin-cornea protocols are considered; "it can't be done" is often not the final answer.
  • Is the effect permanent? In the great majority, yes; rarely, teenagers show renewed progression years later and may need a second treatment. This is why follow-up is never stopped.

What to do at home: the most important treatment is in your hands

Eye rubbing is the strongest and most completely preventable trigger of keratoconus in children. Stopping your child from rubbing is a treatment step as important as cross-linking. The habit cannot be broken unless the cause of the itching (allergy, dry eye, blepharitis) is treated.

  1. Treat the allergy. Keep up the treatment for hay fever, allergic conjunctivitis and eczema arranged by your eye doctor and, if needed, a pediatric allergist. Keep the drops to be used when itching starts at hand.
  2. Cold instead of rubbing. When itching strikes, a clean cold compress is applied for a few minutes with the eyes closed; pressing with the knuckles is never allowed.
  3. Sleeping position. For children who sleep face-down or with the eye buried in the pillow, arrange back-sleeping; a protective night shield may be suggested.
  4. Hand and eye hygiene. Face washing, lid-margin cleaning and contact-lens hygiene reduce itching.
  5. Talk to your child. "Don't rub your eyes" alone does not work; explain why in age-appropriate terms and ask them to tell you when it itches.

School, sports and screens

A child with keratoconus goes to a normal school, plays sports and can use screens; a few adjustments suffice. Sitting in the front row to see the board, informing the teacher if the prescription is changing, and requesting large print in exams when needed are helpful. Sports are not a problem; goggles in the pool, washing the face after dust and chlorine, and protective eyewear in contact sports are recommended. Screen time itself does not advance keratoconus; dry eye and itching after screens can trigger rubbing, so breaks and artificial tears are useful.

Follow-up schedule

Follow-up in children is more frequent than in adults: corneal tomography every 3–4 months in the first year after diagnosis, and every 6 months if the disease appears stable. After cross-linking, visits are at day 1, week 1, month 1 and months 3, 6 and 12; yearly follow-up then continues until puberty is complete.

Measuring on the same device at each visit matters for reliable comparison. Keep printouts or digital copies of all your child's maps; if you change centers, this archive transfers years of information at a glance.

Should siblings be screened?

Yes. The risk of keratoconus in first-degree relatives is markedly higher than in the general population, and the disease can begin in a sibling without any symptoms. We recommend that siblings of a child diagnosed with keratoconus be screened once with corneal tomography, especially during adolescence, and rescreened every 1–2 years if normal. Treatment in a sibling caught early is usually far simpler.

Scientific basis of this page

The following studies are selected from Prof. Dr. İbrahim Toprak's peer-reviewed publications on keratoconus in children and in its early stages.

  • Characterized the three-dimensional geometry and volume of the cornea in children and adolescents with early keratoconus, showing that the disease can present differently from adults at this age and that diagnosis should be interpreted accordingly.

    Toprak I, Cavas F, Velázquez JS, Alió del Barrio JL, Alió JL. Three-Dimensional Morphogeometric and Volumetric Characterization of Cornea in Pediatric Patients With Early Keratoconus. American Journal of Ophthalmology, 2021. DOI: 10.1016/j.ajo.2020.09.031

  • Showed that light scattering in the epithelium, the cornea's outermost layer, is increased in keratoconus before symptoms appear, identifying a new marker that may support earlier diagnosis.

    Toprak I, Gunes CE. Increased epithelial backscatter: A novel finding in subclinical and clinical keratoconus. Clinical & Experimental Ophthalmology, 2021. DOI: 10.1111/ceo.13996 · PubMed

  • Over one year of follow-up, epithelial backscatter rose before conventional measurements changed; proposed as a tool for catching progression early, particularly in young patients at risk of rapid progression.

    Toprak I, Gunes CE, Martin-Seker C, Parca O. A novel metric to monitor early progression in keratoconus: epithelial backscatter. International Ophthalmology, 2025. DOI: 10.1007/s10792-025-03627-x

Frequently asked questions

My child has keratoconus. Will they go blind?

No. Keratoconus does not cause total blindness. Left untreated it can cause serious visual impairment that glasses cannot correct; with early diagnosis and cross-linking, the great majority of children keep normal vision and quality of life.

At what age can cross-linking be done?

There is no fixed age limit; progression and corneal thickness are what matter. Cases treated from age 8–10 are reported in the literature; in young children the procedure is done under general anesthesia. The decision depends on the child's cooperation and the speed of the disease.

My child refuses glasses. Can they wear contact lenses?

Yes. Soft toric, rigid gas-permeable, hybrid and scleral lenses can be used in children with keratoconus; in advanced stages lenses give far better vision than glasses. Lens hygiene and regular check-ups are essential, since improper lens use can damage the cornea.

Will keratoconus affect my child's school performance?

If unrecognized, yes; a child who cannot see the board clearly and tires when reading may fall behind. With correct glasses or lenses and simple adjustments such as front-row seating, this effect largely disappears.

Does keratoconus stop after puberty?

In most patients progression slows or stops in the 30s, but adolescence is the period of fastest progression. Waiting for it to 'settle after puberty' is not advisable; on the contrary, the closest follow-up belongs in this period.

What happens if my child rubs their eyes again after cross-linking?

Cross-linking strengthens the corneal tissue but does not provide unlimited protection against ongoing mechanical trauma. Continued rubbing after treatment can, rarely, set the stage for renewed progression. Treating the allergy and breaking the rubbing habit are part of the treatment.

Which doctor should follow my child's keratoconus?

A cornea specialist with experience in corneal tomography and cross-linking in children. Centers where pediatric ophthalmology and the cornea service work together offer the ideal setting.
